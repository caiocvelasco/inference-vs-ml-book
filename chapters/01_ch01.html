Chapter 1: Inference vs. Prediction

Motivation

Modeling data is a guess at the truth. Some guess by assuming how the data is generated [Statistics]. Others just try to predict what comes next [Data Science]. (Me!)

The trick to being a scientist is to be open to using a wide varietyof tools. (Leo Breiman, Statistical Modeling: The Two Cultures - 2001)

1. Statistical Modeling: What Does It Mean to Learn from Data?

Statistical Modeling is not just about collecting and processing data — it’s about turning information into understanding or action.

But what does it really mean to “learn” from data? Is it about uncovering the hidden processes that generate the observations? Or is it about accurately predicting what comes next?

This chapter explores two dominant perspectives:

“There are two cultures in the use of statistical modeling to reach conclusions from data: one assumes data are generated by a given stochastic model; the other uses algorithmic models and treats the data mechanism as unknown.”
— Leo Breiman, Statistical Modeling: The Two Cultures (Breiman 2001)

We aim to understand these two cultures: Statistical Inference and Machine Learning, and how they shape the way we build knowledge and make decisions from data.

In general, keep this in mind:

  • Inference: Understanding the world.
  • Prediction: Performing well in the world.

2. The Data Modeling Paradigm: Inference

At the heart of Statistical Inference lies the idea of modeling how data are generated. This process begins not with the data themselves, but with a Stochastic Phenomenon — a process that produces variability and uncertainty in the world. According to Aris Spanos (Spanos 1999, 2010), such phenomena often give rise to Chance Regularities: patterns that emerge not from deterministic rules, but from underlying regular behavior.

In this framework, the goal of a statistical model is to translate observed regularities into a formal structure that summarizes the statistical information present in the data. This allows us not only to describe patterns but also to reason about uncertainty, test hypotheses, and draw conclusions beyond the data.

A crucial step for the researcher is to ask:

“Is the Chance Mechanism underlying this Stochastic Phenomenon explicit or not? If not, is it still amenable to statistical modeling?”

This can be conceptualized as:

Stochastic Phenomenon → gives rise to → Observed Data → which exhibits → Chance Regularity patterns → which is formalized by → Probability Theory → which underpins → the Statistical Model → which is the hallmark of → Statistical Inference.

(Don’t worry, we will define all the terms above.)

This question determines whether the phenomenon can be studied through a well-specified statistical model — the hallmark of statistical inference.

A Broad View of The Inferential Process:

In the statistical modeling framework, inference is the act of learning about the unknown structure — often captured by a probability distribution, which in turn is often summarized by parameters — that governs the data-generating mechanism. This structure is assumed to produce data that exhibit regular patterns (or chance regularities) despite underlying randomness. These regularities are the key to recovering information about the distribution’s parameters, which encode the behavior of the system.

The Inferential Process typically:

  • Starts with (or specifies) a model (e.g., linear regression with errors following a normal distribution).
  • Formalizing assumptions (e.g., independence, distributional form, and constant variance - aka homoskedasticity).
  • Estimating parameters that describe the underlying mechanism.
  • Assessing model fit and uncertainty using tools like
    • Confidence intervals (quantifying estimation precision),
    • Hypothesis Tests (testing formal claims about parameters),
    • p-values (measuring surprise under a null hypothesis)
  • Interpreting results in light of the original assumptions and context.

(Again, don’t worry, we will define all the terms above.)

This process turns observed data into insight about the process that generated it — which is fundamentally different from prediction-driven approaches that may bypass model interpretability entirely.

Example

Estimating the Causal Effect of Education on Income using Linear Regression.

\[ Y_i \text{(Income)} = \beta_0 + \beta_1 \cdot \text{Education}_i + \varepsilon_i \]

Suppose we want to understand how years of education influence an individual’s income. We collect data on a sample of individuals, including their income, years of education, age, and other socioeconomic variables.

In the inferential framework, the goal is not merely to predict income, but to learn about the underlying structure — specifically, the stochastic mechanism by which education affects income. In this context, we are particularly interested in the causal effect of education on income.

We assume that income is generated by a stochastic mechanism in which some variables (like education) systematically influence outcomes, while others introduce random variation and are initially captured in the error term.

The task of Statistical Modeling (os Statistical Inference in this case) is to disentangle these components to make meaningful inferences about the relationship. We model this using a Linear Regression, which is a form of Statistical Model built on the following assumptions:

  • Linearity: The relationship between education and income is linear.
  • Independence: Observations are independent of one another.
  • Homoscedasticity: The variance of income is constant across levels of education.
  • Normality (often assumed for inference): The error terms follow a normal distribution.

These assumptions define the structure of the data-generating process — a probability distribution over income conditional on education and other covariates.

The Data-Generating Process (DGP)

These assumptions define the structure of the data-generating process — typically expressed as a probability distribution over the outcome variable conditional on explanatory variables \(X\):

\[ Y \mid X \sim P(Y \mid X; \theta) \]

Where:

  • \(Y\) is the response or dependent variable (e.g., income),
  • \(X\) is a vector of covariates or explanatory variables (e.g., education, experience),
  • \(\theta\) represents the parameters of the distribution (e.g., regression coefficients).

In the context of a linear regression model, this structure becomes more specific:

\[ Y_i = X_i^\top \beta + \varepsilon_i, \quad \varepsilon_i \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2) \]

Where:

  • \(Y_i\) is the observed outcome (e.g., income for individual \(i\)),
  • \(X_i\) is a vector of covariates (e.g., education, experience),
  • \(\beta\) are the parameters of the model capturing systematic effects,
  • \(\varepsilon_i\) is the error term — capturing random variation or unobserved factors.
  • \(\mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2)\) is one of the assumptions, in this case about the error term.

This model implies that the outcome (Income) is not purely random, nor purely deterministic. It is shaped by systematic structure (through \(X_i^\top \beta\) - Education, for example) and randomness (through whatever is hidden in \(\varepsilon_i\)).

Conclusion

The Data-Generating Process (DGP) is the theoretical framework that describes how observed data arise from underlying stochastic mechanisms. In this example, the DGP specifies that income \(Y\) is influenced by education \(X\) and potentially other covariates. The assumptions about linearity, independence, and normality help define the probability distribution that governs the relationship between these variables.

By understanding the DGP, statistical modeling allows us to make inferences about the parameters (e.g., \(\beta_1\)) and explain how and why education influences income — not just predict future outcomes. Since we’ve imposed structure and assumptions, we are no longer simply associating variables — we are reasoning about the underlying mechanism and drawing interpretable conclusions from data.

3. The Algorithmic Paradigm: Prediction

Machine learning takes a different path. Instead of assuming a model structure, it lets the data speak by fitting complex, flexible functions (e.g., decision trees, neural networks) to minimize prediction error (Hastie, Tibshirani, and Friedman 2009).

Key characteristics: - Focus on generalization to new data. - Model evaluation via test error, cross-validation, and metrics like ROC-AUC or accuracy. - Often treated as a black box, especially when interpretability is not the priority.

Example: Predicting which users will churn in a subscription service.

*It’s worth noting that terminology differs across key thinkers in the field. For example, Leo Breiman, in his influential paper “Statistical Modeling: The Two Cultures” (Breiman 2001), distinguishes between two approaches: the Data Modeling culture, which builds models based on assumptions about how data are generated, and the Algorithmic Modeling culture, which focuses on predictive accuracy without necessarily assuming a stochastic mechanism.

In Breiman’s framing, Data Modeling (e.g., linear regression, logistic regression) is one of the two cultures within the broader Statistical Modeling landscape. However, Aris Spanos, representing a more traditional and formalist perspective in statistics, treats Statistical Modeling as synonymous with the inferential approach — a process firmly grounded in probability theory and guided by assumptions about the Data-Generating Mechanism (Spanos 1999, 2010).

So while Breiman separates Data Modeling from Algorithmic Modeling, Spanos (and much of classical statistics) treats Statistical Inference as the modeling of Stochastic Mechanisms, where the central goal is understanding rather than just predicting.*

4. What Counts as Success?

The two paradigms have different notions of success:

Metric Inference Machine Learning
Model fit Residuals, R² Out-of-sample performance
Uncertainty Confidence intervals Variance via bootstrapping
Interpretation Coefficient significance Feature importance
Causality Explicitly modeled Usually absent

These differences are not cosmetic — they guide how each field is taught, practiced, and deployed in real-world settings.

5. The Tradeoff: When to Use Which?

In the tech industry, ML is often preferred due to its ability to optimize at scale. When the goal is personalization, automation, or speed, prediction accuracy matters more than inference.

But in medicine, policy, or science, you must know why something works. That’s where inference — with its structured assumptions — is indispensable.

Question Best Approach
“Does this drug reduce mortality?” Inference (RCTs, causal models)
“Which users are likely to cancel?” ML (predictive modeling)

This reflects Breiman’s (Breiman 2001) key concern: the data modeling culture often fails in tasks where prediction is the goal.

6. Bridging the Gap: Can They Coexist?

The divide is not always rigid. Recent trends aim to merge interpretability with prediction:

  • Interpretable ML: Shapley values, partial dependence plots.
  • Causal ML: Treatment effect estimation with decision trees (Athey and Imbens 2016).
  • Statistical Learning Theory: Formalizes ML’s generalization focus (Vapnik 1999).
  • GAMs and Bayesian models: Offer middle grounds between black-box ML and strict inference.

Books like:

  • The Elements of Statistical Learning (Hastie, Tibshirani, and Friedman 2009)
  • and An Introduction to Statistical Learning (James et al. 2021) attempt to bridge these views with mathematical clarity.

7. Conclusion: A Data Scientist’s Dilemma

In the modern data landscape, there’s no “one size fits all.”
Statistical inference excels when you need to understand.
Machine learning excels when you need to predict.

The question isn’t “Which is better?”
It’s “What problem are you solving?”
